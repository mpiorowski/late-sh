# Games Context

## Metadata
- Scope: `late-ssh/src/app/games`
- Purpose: shared game-domain primitives and services used across game surfaces (Arcade, the house tables in `app/lobby/house`, and the Daily correspondence domain in `app/lobby/daily`).

## Source Map
- `mod.rs` declares shared game modules only.
- `cards.rs` defines card ranks, suits, `PlayingCard`, and ASCII card rendering themes (`Minimal`/`Boxed` one-line faces, `Outline` five-line faces, plus backs, empty slots, and a stock counter) used by Solitaire, the house card tables, and daily briscola, cribbage, and gin rummy. It renders cards and nothing else: there is no shared deck or shuffle here, and each game owns its own rank set and point values (briscola's ten-rank order generalizes to no other game; daily cribbage and gin share the 52-card deck in `app/lobby/daily/std_deck.rs`).
- `chips/svc.rs` owns the Late Chips economy adapter: login ensure, bet debits, payout credits, floor restore, Activity-driven daily puzzle rewards, and reward-template claims for room-game daily/cooldown/per-event payouts. SQL stays in `late-core` models. Every mutation names a `ChipMove` variant (closed roster in `late-core/src/models/chips.rs`: ledger reason, debit floor, source kind, earnings flag) and flows through `UserChips::apply` or a dedicated path (`restore_floor`, `transfer_gift`, game payout claims); the `chip_user_changed` notify comes from `user_chips` triggers (migration 128), never from call sites.
- `chess_core/` is the surface-agnostic chess kernel (extracted from the demolished rooms chess table; see `devdocs/FRD-DAILY.md`). Daily chess and daily chess960 are its only consumers today, and they differ only in the opening position:
  - `types.rs`: `ChessColor`, `ChessPieceKind`, `ChessPiece`, `ChessGameResult`, `ChessMoveSpec`, `ChessMoveRecord`, `ChessPieceRenderMode`, `piece_glyph`.
  - `rules.rs`: pure helpers over `cozy_chess::Board` (legal move generation, queen-promotion move resolution, SAN labels, piece-array projection, repetition counting), plus `random_chess960_board` (the shuffled back rank, bishops on opposite colours and king between its rooks, mirrored for Black) and `fen`, the one way a position is written down. **Castling has two gestures, one encoding.** cozy-chess is Chess960-native, so it generates a castle as the king capturing its own rook (`e1h1`): select the king, then the rook. `legal_moves` additionally emits the lichess/chess.com pair (the king pushed two squares toward the rook, `e1g1`) for every generated castle, so both squares light up as legal targets, and `legal_move_for` maps that pair back onto the rook square via the board's stored castle rights. **That second pair is offered from e1/e8 only** (`castle_king_landing` and `castle_rook_target` share the guard): a Chess960 king starting anywhere else has ordinary one-step moves onto c1/g1, so there it castles by taking its rook and nothing else. Everything downstream (SAN label, `board.play`, the persisted `move_history` from/to) sees only the king-captures-rook move, so history and last-move highlights stay uniform however the player castled.
  - **FEN is Shredder notation for both variants** (`rules::fen`, i.e. `{board:#}`): castling rights name the rooks' own files, `HAha` where standard FEN writes `KQkq`. `KQkq` cannot name a rook that does not stand on a1/h1, so a Chess960 position written that way does not read back at all; one notation for both means no caller has to know which variant it is holding. `str::parse` accepts either spelling, so FENs stored before the switch keep loading.
  - `board_ui.rs`: the tiered board renderer (`Tier`/`pick_tier`, `BoardCtx`, `draw_board`, mouse `square_at`, `king_square`). Callers pass a plain `[Option<ChessPiece>; 64]` plus display context, never a table snapshot; piece-graphics image ids derive from a caller-supplied `placement_seed` Uuid (daily passes `match_id`; other surfaces pass their own stable id).
  - `piece_art.rs`: embedded PNG piece graphics for Kitty/iTerm2/Sixel plus tier thresholds.
  - `cursor.rs`: orientation-aware board cursor movement and legal-target filtering.
- `pool_core/` is the surface-agnostic billiard kernel behind daily eight-ball and nine-ball (`app/lobby/daily/pool.rs`). Like `chess_core` it knows nothing about matches, rows or screens, so a live pool hall as a house table would reuse it whole. **No new dependencies**: there is no mature billiards crate in Rust, and `rapier2d` is not one — a 2D rigid body carries a scalar rotation, and draw/follow is spin about a *horizontal* axis, so it cannot express them at all.
  - `table.rs`: `TableSpec`, every dimension and coefficient in SI — which is why snooker turned out to be a constant plus a ruleset rather than a rewrite. Presets `BAR_BOX_7FT` (pool's default, an amateur bar box), `PRO_9FT`, and `SNOOKER_12FT`, resolved by name through `preset()`. The spec also carries the baulk line and the D, because a snooker baulk line is not a quarter of the table and the rule and the painted marking have to be one number. **One** geometry builder emits cushions and pocket mouths together so they cannot drift apart; a pocket is the chord between its jaw tips and potting is the centre crossing it, so there is no capture radius to tune and a ball can never be both on the table and in a pocket.
  - `ball.rs`: ball state and the four motion states (stationary / spinning / sliding / rolling), each evolved in closed form. 2D velocity paired with **3D** angular velocity — the Han (2005) model `pooltool` implements, and the pairing is the whole point. Rest epsilons here are *physical* (0.1 mm/s), not floating-point: friction is asymptotic, so round-off-scale thresholds mean balls creep forever and a settling cluster generates contacts without the clock advancing.
  - `collide.rs`: ball-ball (with cut-induced throw) and ball-cushion impulses. `throw` is the ball-ball algebra done once per unit speed, so the aim can ask where a thrown object ball really goes; the throw is a few degrees at any speed because the slip and the friction cap both scale with it, and gearing english cancels it. `cushion_deflection` is the cushion's twin and the aim's answer to "does english do anything off a rail": it does, up to about 25° on a full tip, and the drawn rebound shows it. It is deliberately **not** a second model: it pushes a unit-speed rolling ball into the cushion through `ball_cushion` itself and measures the angle back from the mirror, so a retuned coefficient cannot leave the picture behind. Both cushion impulses stay in the tilted contact frame; resolving the normal along the horizontal while building friction against the contact normal *creates energy*, which showed up as a strike settling into bouncing between two rails forever.
  - `sim.rs`: the shot loop and the determinism guarantee. Motion inside a step is the exact closed form; only the contact *time* is numeric, by bisecting a boolean predicate for a **fixed 40 iterations** — no convergence test, so the work done is a pure function of the state. The textbook ball-ball quartic is near-double-rooted on grazing approaches, which is exactly the tunnelling and `t≈0` stall case. No `HashMap`/`HashSet` anywhere (iteration order must be positional), no `rand` (that lives in `rack.rs` behind a seed), every loop bound a constant.
  - `aim.rs`: the shot line. An aim is a **bearing** from the cue ball and nothing else; `shot_line` walks it across the table and hands back everything a renderer draws and every number a readout shows: the first ball it reaches and the ghost ball there, the first `OBJECT_GUIDE_REACH` (0.13) of the table's length along the leg the object ball leaves on, or less where it meets something (short on purpose: it shows the direction, never whether the shot pots), the cue ball's stun line off the contact (`TANGENT_STUB`, also a share of the table so the two lines keep their proportions whatever the table), or the rail a miss meets and where the cue ball comes off it. Straight-line geometry plus the two pieces of physics that would otherwise make the line lie, both of which turn on the strike's `tip_side`: the object ball's leg leaves along `collide::throw`, since a cut drags the ball a few degrees toward the cue ball's travel and over a table's length that is more than a pocket forgives; and the rebound leaves along the mirror *turned by* `collide::cushion_deflection`, since english off a rail is worth twenty degrees in the simulation and a plain mirror would draw none of it. `first_ball` is the ball the cue ball will actually touch, which is not the ball the line is *sighted* on — an aim running past a ball into the rail behind it contacts nothing — and only that one can be judged a foul, which is what the board rings in red. `cast` treats a cushion as its segment *and* its two jaw tips as one-radius bumpers, exactly as the resolver does, so the line rattles where the table rattles instead of reporting a clean drop. Cheap enough to redo on every pointer event, and the reason the draft stores no target: which ball the aim is on is read back off the line (`sighted`, within `SIGHT_REACH` radii so the cue panel keeps showing a ball while the aim is walked off its edge). `aim_test` checks the object leg's direction against the simulator rather than against the line's own geometry. There is no pot-finding aid: nothing in the kernel computes the bearing that pots a ball. Signs follow the screen: a positive sighted offset and a positive cut are to the *right* as seen from behind the cue ball, which is how the cue panel and the eye view draw them.
  - `cue.rs`: the strike model (level cue, so no masse and no jumps), the miscue limit, `PowerBand` (light / normal / strong, each a ceiling the pointer's travel is spread across — a terminal has a few dozen rows to spend, and stretching 0-12 m/s over them puts a safety and a break a few pixels apart), and `ShotMode` — what the pointer is currently steering, with the hint strings the board shows verbatim. **Modes, not stages**: nothing about a shot is sequenced, and the reason it is a mode rather than a held key is that a terminal reports no key-up without the Kitty keyboard protocol. Both live here rather than in the board screen because they are part of the shot *model*, not one surface's input handling.
  - `shot.rs`: `Shot` (the persisted inputs), `RackState`, `ShotOutcome` (what the rules layer sees — deliberately free of physics types), and `Timeline`/`sample`. Timelines are never persisted: a shot is thousands of steps, and re-deriving beats storing. `Timeline::cue_launch` reads where the cue ball stood and which way it left back off the frames rather than off the `Shot`, because a replay plays several shots in a row and the camera needs to know which of them is on screen. `ShotOutcome::cushion_after_contact` is recorded and nothing in the rules layer reads it: the no-rail foul is "no rail at all" (see `rules.rs`), and the field is kept because it is the one thing separating a safety played *into* a rail from one played off it.
  - `rack.rs`: seeded racks, and the only place randomness enters — its own SplitMix64, because `rand` offers no cross-version stability guarantee and a stored seed has to mean the same thing next release.
  - `rules_snooker.rs`: the full frame — fifteen reds, colours re-spotted while a red is up then taken in order, real foul values, play-again, and free ball. **`legal_targets` asks `on_colour` before it asks about the reds**, and that order is the rule: the last red earns a colour of the striker's choice exactly like every other red, and only once *that* colour is down (re-spotted, so `balls_to_spot` keys off `reds_left || on_colour`) do the six come back starting at the yellow. Asking about the reds first would make the final red the one red in the frame that puts the striker on the yellow, and cost them the best ball on the table at the end of every frame. No no-rail foul: snooker has no rule that a ball must reach a cushion after contact, so a roll-up is the legal safety it is on a real table (the pool rulesets keep theirs). **The one ruleset that scores**, which is why `Ruling` has carried `points` and `balls_to_spot` since before there was anything to use them. Two decisions worth knowing: **nomination is inferred** (the ball you hit first is the one you nominated, which is how the game is played among friends, is unambiguous after the fact, and makes free ball need no new input), and **snookered is computed by the caller** (`is_snookered` tests the two grazing paths to each ball on rather than the line between centres — the rule asks whether *both extreme edges* are covered, and a ball you can only clip is still a ball you can hit). That split is in the signature: `judge` takes no `snookered` argument and a foul returns `free_ball_if_snookered`, because the table that settles it is the one *after* `balls_to_spot` goes back up, which only the caller can build (`DailyPoolState::table_after`). Handing this layer a bool computed from the pre-spot rack is the bug the shape now prevents. The other trap the tests pin: `legal_targets` is the list of balls you may *hit*, not the ball that is *on* — in the on-a-colour phase it is every colour, so reading it as the ball on scores two colours potted together and prices every foul at the black. Snooker's balls take their own id range, 16..=30 reds and 31..=36 colours ascending by value, so nothing is ambiguous across rulesets: a pool ball's id is its printed number, and a snooker ball has no number.
  - `rules.rs` + `rules_eight.rs` / `rules_nine.rs`: `PoolRules`, a closed enum with exhaustive matches like the daily roster. The rules layer sees only `GameState` and `ShotOutcome`, never a physics type, so every ruleset is testable from struct literals with no table and no simulation. `Ruling` carries `points` and `balls_to_spot` from day one so snooker is a third file plus a variant, not a trait change. **The no-rail foul is the lenient reading** (`stalled`): a rail *anywhere* in the shot counts, not only one reached after the cue ball's first contact. The book rule is the stricter one; the shape it catches most often is the cue ball off a rail and then thinly onto the ball on, which is a clean safety on any table anybody plays on, and a rule that is right on paper and wrong on the shots people play is the worst kind to keep.
  - `canvas.rs`: a half-block drawing surface (`▀` with fg/bg, so pixels come out near-square). Same trick as `app/lobby/house/image_render.rs`, but a surface you draw *into* rather than an image converter, and it lives here because `games` may not depend on `lobby`.
  - `table_3d.rs`: the shooter's-eye view of the whole table, `v` on the board. **A raycaster, not a 3D renderer**: everything worth seeing lies on one plane, so instead of projecting geometry it walks the *screen* and asks each pixel what it is looking at — cast a ray, hit the cloth, look up the spot. That removes near-plane clipping, the polygon rasteriser and the horizon special-case in one go (rays above the horizon simply never hit the plane), and it hands back the reverse mapping the mouse needs for free: `Eye::to_table` is an exact twin of `View::to_table`, not an approximation. Only the balls are projected, since they are the only things standing off the cloth; they are painted far to near so a ball in front covers one behind it, which is the point of the view. Each is a **sprite standing on its contact point** rather than a disc projected from its centre: the vertical is scaled harder than the horizontal, so a ball's own three centimetres of height came out as several pixels of lift and balls near the far rail floated clean over it into the room. Balls come from `table_ui::paint_ball` so they look like the same balls in both.
  - `table_ui.rs` / `cue_ui.rs`: the table overview and the shooter's-eye cue panel. The panel's balls and its sighting line take a *share* of the panel rather than a fixed size, and the board gives the panel a share of the board (`pool_ui::panel_width`), so the one part of the screen that exists to be looked at closely grows with the terminal. Balls are drawn **larger than life** with true positions — a true-scale ball is under two pixels. The `Overlay` (the eye view's `Sight` is the same type) carries the shot line, the set of balls the striker may hit first (`BallSet`) and the called pocket, and `Overlay::look` turns those into how each ball is painted so the two views cannot disagree: the ball the line is on is ringed — **`GUIDE` if hitting it is legal, `FAULT` red if it is not**, the fastest answer on the board to "may I hit this?". The ring goes *round* the ball rather than recolouring its rim: the rim is a shaded edge of the ball's own hue and is what keeps a cluster reading as separate balls, so spending it on the highlight costs more than the highlight is worth — hence `ring_outside`, which is flush by construction. Balls the striker may not hit are pulled toward the cloth, and **every ball that has a number wears it, all the time** (`printed_label`, `label_ink`: white ink on both black balls; shared by the table, the eye view and the cue panel). Numbering only the striker's balls would be quieter, and in nine-ball, where exactly one ball is ever a legal target, it would leave the rest a rack of anonymous coloured dots with the whole point of the game (which ball comes next) unreadable. `printed_label` already keeps the cue ball and the fifteen identical reds bare, so this adds a number only where there is something to tell apart, and `ink_for` pulls the ink toward the cloth with the ball so the striker's still read first. `text_colour` is the other half of that: a ball's own hue as *text*, for the board's list of what is on. Not the same as the hue it is painted with — the 8 and the snooker black are near-black by design, which reads as a ball on green cloth and as nothing at all as a character on a dark terminal — so anything under a luma floor is lifted toward white, and everything already readable is left exactly as painted, or the list and the balls it names stop matching. The line's legs are styled by one table (`leg_style`): the cue ball's leg brightest, the object ball's in its own hue, the stun line and the rebound dimmer, since they are consequences rather than the choice. The exaggeration is a fixed number of *pixels* added rather than a factor multiplying, because a factor keeps inflating forever: a terminal big enough to draw the table properly ended up with balls half again as large as the table said. Adding a pixel and a half doubles a two-pixel ball and is a rounding error on a twelve-pixel one, and it means the renderer never has to know which game it is drawing. A solid is a plain disc and a stripe is a coloured core in a **white rim** (`STRIPE_RIM`, never under a pixel, or on the small balls it falls between two pixel centres and there is no rim at all). The real look, the hue as a band with white ends, broke the disc's silhouette on a ball a few pixels wide into a coloured square with white ears; a rim keeps every ball round. Separation between neighbours (a fresh rack has every disc overlapping) is the ball's own outermost shell, shaded in its own hue — an edge, not an outline. It must live *inside* the disc: a ring of cloth painted just outside each ball separated them beautifully in isolation and had every ball carve a bite out of the neighbour drawn before it, so a cluster came out as rectangles with the stripes hanging off the side. The physics always uses the true radius; nothing in either file feeds back into the simulation. The table keeps its aspect ratio, so whatever the fit leaves over is painted `SURROUND` by `draw_bed` itself rather than left to the caller's background colour — as cloth it read as a green table of the wrong shape. `cue_ui::draw` returns a `PanelHit` (target ball, cue ball, their radii, the settable face's radius, and the first row that belongs to the cue) because the panel's layout is also the board screen's click map: the part of the shot you click is the mode you arm, and every number the hit test needs is read off the drawing rather than recomputed. **The panel does not ring its ball; it says `Invalid Target` in red under it, and only that.** The panel's ball is drawn alone and enormous with nothing beside it for a coloured edge to be read against — which is precisely what makes the same mark work on the table, where the ring says *this one, not those* — so there it reads as styling. Words cannot. Nothing is said about a legal target or a cushion: the panel speaks up only when the shot as aimed is a foul. The cue ball's miscue ring is drawn **only while `ShotMode::Spin` is armed**, for the same reason: a grey circle inside a white ball reads as part of the ball rather than as a boundary, and drawn full time it would be the busiest thing on a panel whose job is to show one ball clearly. The limit is unchanged either way. The cue ball's face there is drawn **magnified** — a true-to-life face spends most of a small ball drawing the part nobody may strike — but only as far as `TIP_FACE` of the drawn ball, not to its rim: magnifying all the way out would put maximum screw on the ball's last pixel, which reads as "the edge is the limit" and makes every extreme a click away. The rim outside the usable face is still the ball, so a click there clamps to `MISCUE_LIMIT` rather than falling through. `CUE_GAP` is the clear air between the bottom of the ball and the top of the cue — a drawing *and* a hit-test number, one constant, because if the two zones touched, a click aimed at the bottom of the face and landing a row low would arm the stroke. **A tall panel spends its extra rows below the cue ball, not above it**: the balls are sized off the panel's *width*, and the cue ball's descent is capped in absolute pixels as well as by its fraction of the height, so once the sighting line is long enough to read every further row goes to the cue — which is the part that moves, and the part the draw-and-push gesture is read off. The cloth's chalk — baulk line, the D, the two spots — is a *predicate* (`marking_at`) sampled by both renderers rather than drawn twice: the overview walks its cloth pixels and the eye view already walks rays, so neither needs to know how to draw a line and the two cannot disagree about where the baulk line is. That line is the only one that means anything: it is `rules::head_string`, the same number the kitchen rule is enforced against. `ring` is the outer shell of the disc of the same radius and `ring_outside` the band immediately beyond it, both over one `annulus` sharing `disc`'s pixel-centre test rather than walking a circle by angle. **One edge test, or the edge leaks, in three ways.** Walking a circle by angle is half a pixel off and disagrees with `disc` about which pixels are the edge, which shows as cloth-coloured dots inside a ball's rim. Asking `ring` for `radius + 1.2` fills from `radius + 0.2` outward, so the pixels between belong to neither and the same dots appear *outside* the rim. And erasing a rectangle's overshoot by testing a pixel at `dx + 0.5` from the centre while writing to `round(cx + dx)` touches two different pixels whenever a ball's centre is not on a whole one, which eats a column down one side of the ball and leaves squared corners standing on the other. The rule that falls out: **never add a fraction to a radius and hope**. Take the radius the ball is drawn at and say how thick the band is (`ring_outside`), or fill only the pixels the disc owns (`stripe_caps`). Tests pin both at ten radii and four sub-pixel offsets. Rails scale with the table (`RAIL_BALLS`, so `fit_area` runs its fit twice — once to learn a ball's size, once with the rail that follows from it), and **every pocket is drawn on the table's edge, straddling it**: centred where its own mouth chord meets the rail, radius reaching its own jaw tips. A corner's chord is the *diagonal* across the corner, so a corner is drawn on the corner point — `mouth/2` further out along the chord's normal — or it floats a ball's width inside the cloth. Ball numbers are painted from a 3x5 pixel font at the largest whole scale that fits inside the disc, because a borrowed terminal glyph is drawn at the cell's size however big the ball is. The font literals are written **high bit leftmost** so each one reads on the page the way it lands on the ball — the first cut had it the other way round and the `1` came out mirrored. Whether a rack paints at all is one decision for the whole rack (`PAINT_BALL_PX`), not per ball: every ball is drawn the same size, so deciding per ball against its own digits put painted numbers on the single-digit balls and terminal glyphs on the two-digit ones. Two digits spilling slightly past the edge is the smaller ugliness.

## Boundaries
- `games` must not depend on `arcade` or `lobby`.
- `arcade` owns solo Arcade screen/runtime/UI.
- `chess_core` and `pool_core` here own only rules, shared types, and the bare renderers; the daily domain owns match lifecycle/persistence and the board screen chrome.
- Shared primitives belong here only when more than one game surface needs them.
- Do not move house-table registries, table settings, or runtime state into `app/games`. Those are Lobby-owned; `app/games` is only for cross-domain primitives/services such as cards, chips, and the chess kernel.
